# Sourcing Playbook — Finding Authority on Purpose

Load this before Phase 1 (SOURCE) when the topic is unfamiliar or the first search
pass returns mostly MIXED/LOW results. The goal is to reach the *origin* of a fact,
not the most clickable summary of it.

---

## The core move: walk upstream

Open-web search optimizes for findability. The page that ranks first is usually a
paraphrase of a paraphrase. For any important claim, keep asking **"where did this
come from?"** until you reach one of:

- a standards body / governing authority
- a peer-reviewed study or its dataset
- official documentation from the maker of the thing
- the named expert who established the idea
- a primary record (court filing, legislation, filing, spec sheet)

Cite the upstream origin, not the blog that restated it.

---

## Authority-biased search patterns

Bend queries toward primary material instead of marketing pages:

- Append: `standard`, `specification`, `spec sheet`, `whitepaper`, `peer reviewed`,
  `documentation`, `technical manual`, `datasheet`, `filetype:pdf`
- Add the governing body or inventor by name once you know it (e.g. the standards
  org, the original manufacturer, the textbook author)
- Restrict domains: standards orgs, `.gov`, `.edu`, the manufacturer's own site,
  recognized journals. Use the search tool's domain filters when available.
- Search the *person*: once you find who wrote the book/standard, search their name
  directly for primary writing.
- Find the document everyone paraphrases: take a distinctive sentence several blogs
  share and search it to locate the common ancestor.

## Domain-specific entry points

| Domain                  | Go here first (TRUSTED/RELIABLE)                                         |
|-------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Engineering / building  | ICC-ES, ASTM, ISO, IEEE, AISC; manufacturer engineering manuals; code   |
| Medicine / health       | Peer-reviewed journals, PubMed, Cochrane, WHO/CDC/NIH, FDA labeling      |
| Law                     | Statutes, regulations, court opinions (primary records), bar associations |
| Software / protocols    | RFCs, official docs, language/library specs, source repos, standards    |
| Science                 | Peer-reviewed papers + datasets, preprint servers, the original study   |
| Finance / economics     | Regulator filings, primary data series, central-bank/agency publications |
| Products / hardware     | Manufacturer datasheets, official manuals, the inventing company        |
| Trades / construction    | Codes, standards, manufacturer install specs, university extension      |

Trade press and university extension content are usually RELIABLE bridges when the
primary source is too dense — but cite the primary for any number or rule.

---

## Handling blocked, paywalled, or unfetchable primaries

High-tier sources are frequently 403, login-walled, or paywalled, while content
farms load freely. **Do not let accessibility pick your sources.**

When a primary source won't load:
1. Record the full citation anyway (title, author, org, ID/DOI/standard number).
2. Look for an open path: author preprint or self-host, the agency's own copy, a
   cached/mirror version, the abstract + metadata.
3. If only metadata is reachable, you may still cite it as the reference and assign
   tier on provenance — flag that the full text wasn't fetched.
4. Never silently swap in a free LOW source as the citation. If a content farm is all
   that loaded, report the claim as *unverified pending the primary source*, named.

A blocked TRUSTED source outranks an open LOW source. Every time.

---

## Multimedia sourcing

- Prefer the manufacturer's / standards body's / credentialed practitioner's own
  channel over aggregator or ad channels.
- Look for: a named presenter with credentials, original footage (lab, install,
  test), on-screen numbers and methodology, references to standards.
- Treat faceless voiceover-over-stock-footage "explainers" as LOW until proven
  otherwise.
- Grade transcripts and on-screen claims with the same rubric as text, and
  corroborate load-bearing claims the same way.

---

## When to stop searching

Stop when you can answer:
1. **Who originally established this?** (you have the primary or its citation)
2. **Is it corroborated?** (independent agreement for load-bearing claims)
3. **What's the residual uncertainty?** (named, with the source that would resolve it)

If you can't answer #1 after a focused pass, say so plainly and present the best
MIXED material *as* MIXED — never dressed up as settled fact.
