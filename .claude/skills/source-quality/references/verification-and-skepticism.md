# Verification, Stress-Testing & Calibrated Trust

Load before Phase 4 (STRESS-TEST) when a claim is high-stakes or when sources agree but
something feels too clean. This file is about the difference between *sources agree* and
*the claim is actually true*, and about doubting at the right intensity.

---

## Calibrated skepticism: doubt proportional to stakes

Skepticism is a dial, not a switch. Turn it up where being wrong is expensive; turn it
down where verification costs more than the claim is worth.

| Claim type | Scrutiny | Bar to accept |
|------------|----------|---------------|
| Safety, structural, legal, medical, financial | Maximum | TRUSTED primary + independent corroboration + stress-test |
| Numbers, specs, dated facts | High | Two independent sources; check recency |
| General domain explanation | Moderate | One reliable source, sanity-checked |
| Trivially verifiable / common knowledge | Low | Don't waste cycles proving the obvious |

**Unreasonable skepticism looks like:** rejecting well-corroborated facts, demanding
primary proof for the trivial, re-litigating settled questions, or never committing.
**Reasonable skepticism looks like:** asking who benefits, checking a second source,
verifying the number, and then *acting* once the evidence clears the bar for the stakes.

The failure mode in both directions is the same: ignoring the evidence in front of you.
Gullibility accepts it; reflexive doubt refuses to weigh it. Calibration weighs it.

---

## The stress-test questions

Run the surviving load-bearing claims through these. Scale the rigor to stakes.

1. **Recency** — Dated? Current, or superseded by newer work? Conventions go stale.
2. **Edge behavior** — Does it hold at the boundaries, or only the happy path? What
   conditions, exceptions, or ranges does the source quietly omit?
3. **Falsification** — What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Go look for that,
   not just for confirmation.
4. **Echo vs. independence** — Do the corroborating sources reason separately, or all
   trace to one origin? Three echoes of one press release is one source, not three.
5. **Incentive** — Who benefits if this is believed? A vendor's claim about its own
   product is data, but weight it and seek a disinterested check.
6. **Mechanism** — Is there a plausible *why*, or just an asserted *what*? Claims with a
   sound mechanism survive better than correlations with none.
7. **Proof vs. polish** — Separate "is shown" from "sounds right." Persuasive writing,
   confident tone, and clean round numbers are not evidence.

A claim that survives the relevant subset is usable. One that only *reads* well gets
downgraded, corroborated harder, or dropped.

---

## Continuous improvement: keep the knowledge current

Treat what we know as living, not fixed.

- **Prefer current research** over older convention unless the older work is the
  established primary and nothing has superseded it.
- **Re-check when stakes are high or time has passed** — a fact verified a year ago may
  have moved.
- **Update on better evidence.** When new, better-sourced information contradicts what we
  used before, change the answer and state plainly what changed and why. Defending a
  prior answer against better evidence is the opposite of this skill's purpose.
- **Record the residual uncertainty** so the next pass knows where the soft spots are.

Getting it right beats being consistent with an earlier mistake.
