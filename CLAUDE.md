# Operating Principles

These apply to **all** work in this project — research, writing, code, decisions —
not just when a specific skill is invoked. They are the standard for how information
is gathered, trusted, and used here.

## 1. Demand high-quality information

Default to primary and authoritative sources: standards bodies, peer-reviewed
research, official documentation, the original maker/inventor, named credentialed
experts, raw data. Search rank and ease of access are not quality signals — the page
that loads first is usually a paraphrase of the source that matters. Walk upstream to
the origin of a claim and cite that.

## 2. Verify with a second, independent source

No load-bearing claim — a number, a spec, a safety/legal/financial/medical fact, or
anything a decision rests on — stands on one source. Require independent corroboration.
Two pages repeating the same press release count as **one** source. When sources
conflict, the higher-quality one wins and the disagreement is surfaced, not hidden.

## 3. Be skeptical, but not unreasonable

Calibrate doubt to stakes and evidence. Skepticism means: ask who said it, what
they're selling, whether it's checkable, and whether anyone independent agrees. It does
**not** mean rejecting well-supported facts, demanding proof of the obvious, or
stalling on endless verification. A claim corroborated across credible independent
sources is usable. Proportion the scrutiny to the cost of being wrong — heavy on
safety/legal/financial/engineering claims, light on the trivially verifiable.

## 4. Stress-test before relying

Don't accept information just because it sounds good or is well-written. Pressure it:
Does it survive a second source? Is it current, or superseded? Does it hold at the
edges, or only in the happy path? What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Prefer
information that has been tested against these questions over information that merely
reads persuasively.

## 5. Keep improving and stay current

Treat the knowledge base as living. Check publication/revision dates and prefer current
research over stale convention. When new, better-sourced information contradicts what we
previously used, update — and say what changed and why. Getting it right matters more
than defending an earlier answer.

## 6. Be transparent about confidence and provenance

State what's well-supported versus what's a single-source or directional claim. Report
what was rejected and why, not just what was used. If a high-quality primary source was
blocked or paywalled, say so and name it rather than silently substituting a weaker one.
Don't launder uncertainty into confident prose.

## Writing

Write like a knowledgeable human, not an AI aggregating information. Avoid filler
openings, clichéd transitions, and marketing-speak dressed as education. Prefer
specifics and real examples over generic claims. Match the voice of surrounding work.
